<p style="color: #000000;"><img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/99/P.T._Barnum%27s_Famous_%22Jumbo%22%2C_ca_1900.jpg" alt="Photo of P.T. Barnum&rsquo;s Famous &ldquo;Jumbo&rdquo; stuffed and on display at the Barnum Museum of Natural History around 1900" width="100%" height="100%" /></p>
<p style="color: #000000;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Let's talk about the elephant in the room<span style="color: #000000;">. By the time <em>Barnum and Jumbo's ABC</em> was published, the popular pachyderm was long since stuffed and put on a pedestal, though it would be several years before its remains came to rest in a stationary exhibit at the P.T. Barnum Museum at Tufts University in Boston. Jumbo's giant footsteps from living spectacle to ephemeral information at a cultural heritage institution is exemplary of many lesser collected things, such as oddly shaped and tattered children's books.&nbsp;</span></span></p>
<details style="color: #000000;">
<summary><span style="color: #000000;"><span style="font-size: 18pt;"><strong>See side show</strong></span><br /><span style="font-size: 18pt;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Click here and be amazed by the world's biggest liar</span>&nbsp;</span></span></summary>
<p><img style="float: right; padding-left: 10px;" src="PT Barnum and Commodore Nutt (1861).jpg" alt="PT Barnum and Commodore Nutt (1861)" width="25%" height="25%" data-api-endpoint="https://canvas.instructure.com/api/v1/courses/12599755/files/307861154" data-api-returntype="File" /><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Until the 1880s, Phineas Taylor "P.T." Barnum, the Mayor of Bridgeport, CT and member of the Connecticut House of Representatives, was not really known as a circus guy, much less a politician. Barnum bought Scudder's American Museum in 1842 and reopened it as The American Museum. It had a bit of anything: beluga whales, Siamese twins, a flea circus, trained bears, and a 3-foot tall little person called General Tom Thumb. Confederate agents burnt the museum down in 1865, and most of the poor animals died in the massive conflagration. Its replacement, Barnum's New Museum, also burned down three years later.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-size: 14pt;">But we know P.T. Barnum from "The Greatest Show on Earth," Ringling Brothers, Barnum &amp; Bailey Circus. Barnum lent his twisted business acumen to a growing craze, and brought his menagerie and sideshows on a modernized circus train to a rapidly expanding post-Civil War re-United States.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><img style="padding-right: 10px; float: left;" src="https://prod.ctda.dgicloud.com/iiif/2/L19mb3htbC9kYXRhc3RyZWFtLzYwMDAyJTNBMTIzNyUyQkpQMiUyQkpQMi4x~drupal_d8_default/28,38,3606,2729/full/0/default.jpg" alt="Print: &quot;P. T. Barnum's New and Greatest Show on Earth coming by Four Special Trains&quot;" width="371" height="274" />Barnum's biggest stunt was his purchase of Jumbo, an African elephant, for $10K from the London Zoo in 1882. As far as celebrities go, Jumbo was the biggest of them all. He had been captured 20 years earlier in Sudan, and sold from Germany to France before landing in England. The British public adored him and didn't want to give him up. Once state-side, Jumbo quickly became the star attraction of Barnum's circus.&nbsp;</span><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Jumbo met his earthly end in early 1885 when he was struck by a speeding freight train. The show must go on, insisted Barnum, and he sent Jumbo's corpse off to Ward's Scientific Establishment in Rochester, NY to be stuffed and returned inside and out to the circus for posthumous labor.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><img style="float: right; padding: 0px 0px 10px 10px;" src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7c/Jumbo_the_elephant_-_3b52419u.jpg/1280px-Jumbo_the_elephant_-_3b52419u.jpg" alt="Poster of Jumbo the elephant's skeleton on display in PT Barnum's circus" width="369" height="277" /></p>
<p><span style="color: #000000; font-size: 14pt;"><img style="float: left; padding: 0px 10px 0px 0px;" src="https://www.theodorerooseveltcenter.org/media/Images/Blog/2019/Akeley-Leopard.jpg" alt="Carl Akeley, wounded and bandaged in colorized photograph with hanging dead leopard (Somalia, 1896)" width="224" height="262" /></span></p>
<p><span style="color: #000000; font-size: 14pt;">There's more than one way to skin a cat, they say, but you'd better make sure it's dead. The cutter &nbsp;on hand at Ward's was Carl Akeley, who was about to disrupt the moribund field of taxidermy. From the Italian Renaissance Akeley developed an appreciation for flayed human bodies taking mannerist poses in sculpture and fresco. </span><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><span style="color: #000000;">As for technique, from the </span><span style="color: #000000;">French he borrowed papier-machet with shellac. Akeley's signature style can be seen in natural history museums across the land, such as </span><span style="color: #000000;">The </span>Field Museum. As for Jumbo, Barnum eventually donated his taxidermied remains to Tufts University in 1889 along with a (scientific) museum in his name. It burned down in 1975 and all that remains of Jumbo is a bit of trunk and his tail. </span><img src="https://www.datocms-assets.com/44232/1644895049-gn92050_028bd.jpg?" alt="Fighting elephants exhibit at the Field museum in Chicago" /></p>
</details>

{%- include heading name="steps" -%}


<details style="color: #000000;">
<summary><span style="color: #000000; font-size: 14pt;"><strong>Adding a IIIF item to Mixtape</strong><br />To contribute an item to a teaching collection <span style="color: #236fa1;"><em>(click to open and close)</em></span></span></summary>
<ol style="list-style-type: decimal;">
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Select item in a digital collection site</span></li>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Locate the IIIF icon or manifest link and click it to open the manifest</span></li>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Copy the manifest URL</span></li>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Paste the manifest URL in the Manifest URL blank in Mixtape, and click Add</span></li>
</ol>
</details>
<details style="color: #000000;">
<summary><span style="color: #000000; font-size: 14pt;"><strong>Cropping a IIIF image</strong><br />To select a portion of an image <span style="color: #236fa1;"><em>(click to open and close)</em></span></span></summary>
<ol style="list-style-type: decimal;">
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Select the Cropping icon in an item thumbnail</span></li>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Click thumbnail to load in the <strong>IIIF Cropping Tool</strong> viewer</span></li>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Zoom and view</span></li>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">To crop a detail from the image, click the white pencil icon, and highlight the desired rectangle by clicking and dragging a rectangle from corner to corner</span></li>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">A thumbnail of your selected detail will appear in a slider at the bottom of the Cropping Tool. You can repeat to collect multiple details.</span></li>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Click the Copy icon on the left side of the thumbnail</span></li>
</ol>
</details>
<details style="color: #000000;">
<summary><span style="color: #000000; font-size: 14pt;"><strong>Posting with an image</strong><br />To add image details to a discussion post <span style="color: #236fa1;"><em>(click to open and close)</em></span></span></summary>
<ol>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">click Reply</span></li>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">to enter your image details, simply paste into the text field</span></li>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">images can be manually resized by highlighting and dragging their corners</span></li>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">repeat for additional images</span></li>
</ol>
</details>

<ol style="list-style-type: decimal;">
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Choose one item/image that you would like to focus on and open it in the Mirador viewer by clicking the M icon</span></li>
<li><span style="font-size: 14pt;">Then open your item in the Cropping tool and crop several details of that image</span></li>

</ol>
