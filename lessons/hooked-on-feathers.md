{%- include heading name="concepts" -%}



<details style="color: #000000;">
    <summary><span style="color: #000000;"><strong>#tag</strong><br />Tagging as "folksonony"&nbsp; <span style="color: #236fa1;"><em>(click to open and close)<br /></em></span></span></summary>
    <p>The #hashtag is a recent invention. It was modestly proposed by a guy named Chris about 20 years ago. Here's how it started:</p>
    <p>&nbsp;</p>
    <p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-size: 12pt; font-family: 'times new roman', times;">"How do you feel about using # (pound) for groups. As in #barcamp [msg]</span></p>
    <p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-size: 12pt; font-family: 'times new roman', times;">&nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp;-Chris Messina</span></p>
    <p>&nbsp;</p>
    <p>Tagging didn't start with Chris Messina. It's been around for a long time. And hashmarks have too. But the combination of symbol and character string changed everything. A hashtag is attached to comments and media so readers can find message topics they are interested in. But you don't need us to tell you that. Everyone knows what a hashtag is. And that is its lure: hash-tagging is accessible to everyone. It converts <em>taxonomy</em> (the imposition of categories onto phenomena) into <em>folksonomy</em> (the popularization of taxonomy). In case you were wondering, the term "folksonomy" was coined by a guy named Thomas Vander Wal in 2004.&nbsp;</p>
</details>
<details style="color: #000000;">
    <summary><span style="color: #000000;"><strong>Metadata as tags</strong><br />Get hooked on tagging &nbsp;<span style="color: #236fa1;"><em>(click to open and close)<br /></em></span></span></summary>
    <p><img src="https://www.nhm.ac.uk/content/dam/nhmwww/our-science/collections/zoology/pipra-manakins-wallace-full-width.jpg.thumb.1920.1920.jpg" alt="Bird skins with tags from the Tring Museum" /></p>
    <p>Even farther back in time, at the dawn of evolution (the 1850s), tags were used to keep track of dead birds. Collectors of animal specimens from around the world, such as Alfred Russel Wallace, used actual paper tags tied with string to the legs of his taxidermied feathered friends to keep track of taxonomy, as well as date, location of origin and other information. Wallace was "the other Darwin," an adventurer contemporary of the famous evolutionary biologist who actually beat Charles to a theory of evolution by natural selection, but was since relegated to footnote status for most of us in favor of the established scientist.</p>

    <p>So, how did we get from flying fish to fishing fly?</p>
    <p><em>The Feather Thief</em>'s account of <em>birds-with-metadata</em> transforming into&nbsp;<em>feathers-with-hooks&nbsp;</em>is really a story about empire and trade. Commodities such as bird feathers derive their monetary, cultural, and/or scientific value from provenance: the tracking system by which people connect an item's current state in presentation to its point of origin. &nbsp;Like the salmon-fly, the hashtag has a business end (the hashmark), and the featherwork.</p>
    <p><strong>To summarize:</strong></p>
    <ul>
        <li>tags connect specimens with metadata</li>
        <li>hashtags snag content and pull it into categories.</li>
    </ul>

        <p>For historical perspective: Wallace and Darwin, two Englishmen, were working out and publishing the theory of evolution at the precise moment &nbsp;that England (with France and the United States) was waging the Second Opium War on Qing China (1856-1860). The biological-geographic expeditions Wallace made to collect specimens in the Amazon River basin (1848-1852) and the Malay Archipelago (1854-1862) were not just providing data for paradigm shifts in scientific knowledge. Like many such expeditions before them, they also generated accurate maps and tabulated data about flora, fauna, people, terrain, etc. that European imperialists would exploit to carve out plantations for valuable commodities and/or traffic indigenous populations for indentured labor. The "value" of Wallace's collections, in other words, is a complex calculation, and balances knowledge against human and ecological destruction.&nbsp;</p>

    <summary><span style="color: #000000;"><strong>Feather / Ihhuitl</strong><br />Consider the example of The Feather in the Digital Florentine Codex&nbsp; <span style="color: #236fa1;"><em>(click to open and close)<br /></em></span></span></summary>
    <p>As vocabulary in the digital edition, <span style="color: #000000;">"feather" and one of its Nahuatl translations "ihhuatl"</span>&nbsp;can be searched for either Text or Image returns. Depending upon which you select, nearly all books in the Florentine Codex are represented in the returns, and the variety of content associated with these search terms is astounding. It is easy to see, in this sense, how pervasive feathers are in Nahua history and culture. Here, the profession of feather seller as two types serves as a kind of metaphor for people in general.&nbsp;</p>

He sells various feathers-precious feathers; he sells fine green feathers, chili-green feathers, this curved at the tip, the feathers of young birds.     <summary><span style="color: #000000;"><strong>Scalar individual page text</strong><br />How to approach your 1500-word text for individual Scalar pages &nbsp;<span style="color: #236fa1;"><em>(click to open and close)</em></span></span></summary>
    <p><span>Think of this not as a traditional research paper that argues a thesis (&ldquo;In this paper I will argue X&hellip;&rdquo;). Instead, this is a visual research project in which you will explain images from the Florentine Codex in relation to Nahua worldviews and cross-cultural exchange on a particular topic. One way to think of this is to imagine that you are giving a tour of an exhibition of the images you have chosen, and are explaining how your visitors can understand how the images and text work together.&nbsp;&nbsp;</span></p>
    <p><strong>Tips</strong></p>
    <ul>
        <li><span>Start by arranging your images in a particular order that makes most sense for your explanation of them.</span></li>
        <li><span>Do an in-depth visual analysis of each image. Describe what you see in each image, taking them apart piece by piece in a formal way, with the goal of revealing the multiple dimensions of the images as both image and a form of text.</span></li>
        <li><span>After your visual analysis, analyze the textual passages and consider when the various texts might add to your contextualization. Make sure to consider the prompts posted above.</span></li>
        <li><span>When possible, focus your attention on the Nahuatl passages first, and then move to the Spanish text. Given that our class is dedicated to thinking about Indigenous forms of knowledge, think carefully about the importance of Nahua worldviews expressed in key terms and ideas in the text.</span></li>
        <li><span>Have a conclusion that wraps up the ideas from your discussion of the individual images and texts.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></li>
    </ul>
    <p><strong>Note on Annotations</strong></p>
    <p><span>Your annotations can come from your 1500-word text, they don&rsquo;t necessarily need to be separate texts. Think of a magazine spread or blog post, where images are explained by quotations from the accompanying text.</span><span>&nbsp;</span></p>
