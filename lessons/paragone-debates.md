<p>The term&nbsp;<em>paragone</em> ("comparison") emerged in an art-historical context from epistolary debates about the relative merits of aesthetic qualities of painting, sculpture, and poetry (as well as architecture, to an extent), during the Renaissance period. Bendetto Varchi (Italian poet, 1502-1565) sparked the conversation in 1546 through letters to cultural practitioners inviting their opinions on the matter. Among them, painters and sculptors, particularly, addressed comparisons of their two respective art forms from conceptual themes to practices and championed their own forms of art as superior to, and distinct from, the other.</p>
<ul>
<li>Painters emphasized the supremacy of sight and their ability to render color and texture to create the illusive perspectives that yields greater potential for naturalistic representation. They also boasted of more liberty in playing around with the objects or figures, reshaping, overstretching, geometricising, dissolving, caricaturing, or meandering, according to the painting's intended effect.</li>
<li>Arguments in favor of sculpture focused on its three-dimensionality, which can better access depth and volume and could be viewed from multiple angles, a method allowing several different and faithful views of the same figure. Since the Renaissance artists attempted a "rebirth" of the culture of classical Greek and Roman art, they often did so through employing the same subject matter or by grappling with technical challenges presented in an antique work, such as representation of <em>contrapposto</em> ("counterpoise," describing a human figure standing with most of its weight on one foot), like in Michelangelo's statue of <em>David</em>, to express a psychological disposition; or representation of <em>figura serpentinta </em>("serpentine figure"), like in the<em> Laoco&ouml;n and His Sons</em> and <em>Rape of a Sabina</em>, featuring figures in a spiral pose that creates more dynamic in showing physical power, passion, tension, and semantic movement.</li>
</ul>
<p>The essential point in the debate to both painting and sculpture, however, lay on the notion of <em>disegno</em> ("the conception of a work"). It led to the influential idea and practice that drawing excels observation of all manner of things and serves as the foundation of all art forms.&nbsp;</p>
