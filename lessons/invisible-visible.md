## {{page.title}}

{%- include heading name="concept" -%}


<p>When you look at a traditional map, you are usually looking at things you could theoretically walk up to and touch: a river, a mountain range, the border of a city. But medical cartography requires mapmakers to do something akin to magic: <strong>they have to make the invisible visible.</strong></p>
<p>You cannot see "cholera" immersing in water. You cannot physically touch a "mortality rate." To put these abstract, terrifying, and invisible threats onto a physical landscape, mapmakers have to invent visual conventions. They have to decide what a disease <em>looks like</em> on paper. Is it a dense cluster of black dots? Is it an angry red shaded region? Or is it represented by a monstrous cartoon mosquito? By studying these formal choices&mdash;the symbols, colors, legends, and illustrations&mdash;we stop reading maps as simple reflections of reality and start reading them as deliberate, subjective arguments designed to persuade, warn, or control.</p>


{%- include heading name="activity" -%}


<p>Your task is to go hunting for the invisible. In this lab, we will get acclimated to our primary sources by focusing entirely on <strong>symbology and formal elements</strong>. We will use the embedded Mixtape viewer to scan the "Maps and Medicine" collection. Instead of looking at the geography, you will look specifically at how the cartographers chose to visualize disease and death, isolating these visual conventions using the cropping tool.</p>
</div>
<div id="kl_custom_block_3" class="">
<h3 class="" style="background-color: #0073a3; color: #ffffff; padding: 0.2em 0.6em; border-radius: 30px;">👓 &nbsp;Examples</h3>
<p>Notice how drastically the "feel" and meaning of a map changes based purely on the formal conventions chosen to represent the data:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Point Data (The Dot Matrix):</strong> Marking individual deaths with dots or bars treats disease as a series of specific, quantifiable tragic events. It turns the map into a forensic crime scene.</li>
<li><strong>Choropleth (Color Shading):</strong> Shading entire regions with dark, ominous colors suggests disease is an environmental miasma&mdash;a dark cloud hanging over a whole population rather than individual cases.</li>
<li><strong>Allegorical/Multimodal:</strong> Sometimes data fails, and mapmakers use illustrative art to anthropomorphize the threat, combining geography with emotional propaganda.</li>
</ul>
