## {{page.title}}

{%- include heading name="concept" -%}


<p><img src="Towser.jpg" />Stephen Colledge was a man of many talents. &nbsp;Called "the joiner," Colledge was best known for his scurrilous graphics, in printed broadsides and playing cards in a form we now categorize as&nbsp;<em>political cartoon</em>, a genre inextricably entwined in the American tradition with the First Amendment to The&nbsp;Constitution, which safeguards free speech.</p>
<p>17th-century England did not have freedom of the press as we know it. But for a brief period beginning in 1679, at the outbreak of an anti-Catholic hysteria and misinformation campaign known as the Popish Plot, London censorship mechanisms were overwhelmed by an explosion in print culture and rapid postal communication and allowed to lapse. Colledge drew, printed and secretly distributed a series of broadsides critical of King Charles II, whom he suspected of harboring pro-Catholic sentiments, if not intentions to convert. Colledge lampooned, as well, the King's attack dog propagandist and former censor, Roger L'Estrange, nicknamed "Towser."</p>
<p>L'Estrange had the last laugh. Colledge was tried for and found guilty of high treason, then hanged, drawn, and quartered on August 31, 1681. The charge was conspiracy to seize the King during a session of Parliament in Oxford, but the real offense was likely his politically-charged broadsides. According to historian Stephanie Koscak,</p>
<p style="padding-left: 40px;"><em>This is a remarkable instance in the history of antigovernment and antimonarchic satire: Colledge is the only individual to have been executed in part for drawing a nasty picture of an English ruler, either before or since.</em>&nbsp;</p>
<p>We are interested in him for yet another reason: his speech bubbles. Graphically, they present as scrolls, but they are meant to be understood as words spoken. In this sense, the lone martyr of English free "speech" helped introduce a form of political participation that continues to inform and bedevil popular governance to this day.&nbsp;</p>


{%- include heading name="activity" -%}

<p>Our job for this activity is to collectively annotate the visual graphic languages of "free" speech at its infancy. As a starting point, we will consider first the simplified, cartoonish linearity of Colledge's images. For reference, below on the left is a formal portrait of King Charles II, painted in 1683-4, roughly the same time that Colledge was executed. On the right is Colledge's caricatured depiction of Charles. Given the advancements in technique, materials, and optics during the European Enlightenment, court-approved artists such as John Riley could represent the middle-aged king with near photographic quality. What function, then, did linear simplification, crudity, and other qualities of Colledge's imagery serve to express political sentiments visually? Further, we will consider the verbal and visual symbols communicated to the various audiences of Colledge's works. These would be viewed and discussed, typically, in London's many coffee houses at the time, so individual readers could draw upon their friends, associates, or fellow conspirators to help decode the meanings. We will not be able to determine all the references, but some are suggested in the British Museum site.&nbsp;</p>
