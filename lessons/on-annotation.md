## {{page.title}}


{%- include heading name="concept" -%}


<p><img src="https://media.getty.edu/iiif/image/7cb00ef5-7b5d-4a78-bf08-0f12ffc6b21e/1534,3118,1766,731/max/0/default.jpg" alt="Pictographic representation of the third lord of Tlatelolco, named Cuauhtlatoa, showing a seated ruler and eagle head" style="width:100%;"/></p>
<p>The W3C (World Wide Web Consortium) writes standards for content on the web. It defines annotation as a relational structure that assigns a "body" (often a brief text comment) to a "target" (typically a highlighted passage of text, or outlined portion of an image). An annotation can be created by an original author of a work, for example, or by a reader who makes a mark in a copy of the work they own (or checked out of the library!)</p>
<p><span style="font-family: inherit; font-size: 1rem;"><strong><img style="float: left; padding-right: 10px;" src="https://www.w3.org/TR/annotation-model/images/intro_model.png" alt="W3C model for annotation: a graphic representation with three connected ovals labeled annotation, source and target, with arrows showing relationship" width="30%" height="30%" />An annotation,</strong> in other words, is a comment and/or other piece of information that is attached by an author or reader to a portion of a target object, such as a line of text in a document, a detail in an image, or a clip of video. Most annotations are short text, but they can also be, or include, media, such as an image.</span><span style="font-family: inherit; font-size: 1rem;"> It is different from metadata, which is information about the item, such as who made it, when, and where.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p>As a concept model, the W3C definition of annotation bears striking resemblance to the pictographic language of the Nahua, suggesting that there is more at play in the practice and history of annotation than simply scribbles in the margins of books. It is an idea that connects. Indeed, annotation offers a way for communities to engage in a secondary act of creation with authors and artists.&nbsp;</p>
<p>In art history, spatial annotation of an image is an important method of inquiry related to scholarly practices of close looking and detail analysis. Across academic disciplines, the<span style="font-family: inherit; font-size: 1rem;">&nbsp;practice of annotating is analytical, or&nbsp;<em>inductive</em>, an incremental step between exhibition and thesis or argument. It makes your media work for you as evidence, rather than illustration (or decoration) to embellish text.</span></p>

<h3 style="color: #000000;">Components of an Annotation</h3>
<p style="color: #000000;"><span>For our final Scalar project, we will focus on a particular construct for annotation that can be used in many different situations. It has three elements:</span></p>
<ol style="color: #000000;">
    <li><span><strong>A title </strong>(in Scalar the title is linked to the annotation as a page)</span></li>
    <li><span>A description or observation (parts 2 and 3 here comprise the "Content" field in the Scalar annotation tool)</span></li>
    <li><span>An interpretive statement<br /></span></li>
</ol>
<p style="color: #000000;"><strong>Example:</strong><span> "</span><strong><span>Simple collar</span></strong><span>. In the painting, the father's collar is mid-sized, white, and has just a bit of lace frill around the edges. It appears to express simple values or Puritanism."</span></p>
<h3 style="color: #000000;">Standard of Plausibility</h3>
<p style="color: #000000;">Annotations express the perspective of the person annotating, but they aren't completely arbitrary. &nbsp;We will apply a basic standard of plausibility, which asks that each contributor consider: <em>originality </em>(the degree to which the annotation expresses an individual, or unique perspective or insight), and <em>possibility&nbsp;</em>(how likely the interpretation is to be valid, from another person's point of view). In the rubric below if either category is a 0, you might want to redo. Any other score is fine.</p>
