## {{page.title}}

{%- include heading name="concept" -%}

<p><a title="detail image" href="https://mcgrawcenter.github.io/mirador/?manifest=https://iiif.library.ucla.edu/ark%3A%2F21198%2Fz12z7gk7/manifest&amp;canvas=https://iiif.library.ucla.edu/ark%3A%2F21198%2Fz12z7gk7/manifest/canvas-ig9e" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><img src="https://iiif.library.ucla.edu/iiif/2/ark%3A%2F21198%2Fz12z7gk7/0,4,1392,880/full/0/default.jpg" alt="Post card feating an off angle view of the University Research Library" width="100%" height="100%" data-manifest="https://iiif.library.ucla.edu/ark%3A%2F21198%2Fz12z7gk7/manifest" /></a> &nbsp;</p>
<p>The University Research Library (URL), later renamed Charles E. Young Research Library (YRL), first opened in late 1964. It was designed by the modernist architect A. Quincy Jones in the "International Style," with rectangular lines of concrete and plate glass enclosing a 5-story box with two basement levels. The library received an addition in 1970, and was renovated in 2009 and 2011. At the time of its construction, the research library realized in physical form the most cutting edge ideas about information and its uses for research and scholarship. The layout of floors, and designation of various library functions in space, such as book storage in shelving stacks, card catalogs, reference services, study spaces, and special collections facilities, help us understand physical space and furnishings as a system of information.&nbsp;</p>


{%- include heading name="activity" -%}

<p>For this Lab Activity, we will enter into the space-time continuum of the Charles E. Young Research Library as a repository of collective memory. Using "mnemonic" techniques developed over several millennia for remembering and recalling facts, ideas, words, and images through spatial memory, we will take a guided tour through YRL, both digitally (via historical photographs from Library Digital Collections), and physically (through your own explorations on foot through the Library's various floors.&nbsp;Along the way, you will learn about digital collections: how they are accessed, browsed, and viewed</p>

<summary style="color: #000000;"><strong>A Brief Digression in Time</strong><br />How Simonides of Keos brought down the house. &nbsp;</summary>
<p style="color: #000000;"><img style="float: right; padding-left: 10px;" src="https://iiif.wellcomecollection.org/image/L0061088/full/,600/0/default.jpg" alt="Schematic image of human memory" width="33%" height="33%" /> <img style="float: left; padding-right: 10px;" src="https://bruinlearn.ucla.edu/courses/202622/files/19374320/preview" alt="A page about the &quot;memory theater&quot; from Robert Fludd's collected works from the early 17th century, with an illustration of the Shakespearean stage" width="33%" height="33%" data-api-endpoint="https://bruinlearn.ucla.edu/api/v1/courses/202622/files/19374320" data-api-returntype="File" />According to legend, the tradition of the memory palace (aka "theater of memory" or "mind palace") traces back to an Ancient Greek disaster with a little cameo on the part of the gods. The lyric poet Simonides of Ceos was doing a gig in Thessaly at a banquet thrown by a local strongman named Scopas. Apparently, Simonides had prattled on too long in tribute to the gods Castor and Pollux, and Scopas impatiently heckled him to get to the point (about himself), to the jeers of his kindred Scopadae and other diners.</p>
<p style="color: #000000;">Next thing we know, there's a knock, and Simonides is called to the door by two young men. He excuses himself, steps outside, and as soon as he does, a massive earthquake rocks Thessaly and the villa is leveled, crushing everyone inside. Only Simonides survives, and in the aftermath he is called upon by forensic investigators to name everyone who was in the room, and where they were sitting. Because he had perfect recall of spatial memory, Simonides was just the man for the job. In retrospect, classicists have concluded that the two young men were none other than Castor &amp; Pollux, who manifested themselves in human form, and crashed the party to exact revenge upon the impertinent Scopadae and company.</p>
<p><span style="color: #000000;">So this one of those topics in Western Civilization where the deeper you dig, the more entangled facts get with mythology. If you're into such things, Frances Yates will tell you all about it in </span><em><span style="color: #000000;"><a class="inline_disabled" href="https://search.library.ucla.edu/permalink/01UCS_LAL/192ecse/cdi_jndl_porta_oai_ndlsearch_ndl_go_jp_R100000136_I1130282270250996480" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><span>The</span> Art of Memory</a> </span></em><span style="color: #000000;">(and if you're a little short on time, you can look at <a class="inline_disabled" title="Link" href="https://exhibitions.lib.cam.ac.uk/reformation/artifacts/a-memory-theatre/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">this Cambridge U. website</a> instead). </span><span style="color: #000000;">Yates is particularly taken with a guy named Robert Fludd, a kooky stage designer and contemporary of William Shakespeare who conjured up a whole cognitive enterprise on the foundations of theatre. Its gist: human spatial memory is indelible. To manage mental content, you tag it with visual symbols (such as a crown for a king), and attach those symbols in your mind's eye to specific locations in a building or other place you know well. </span><span style="color: #000000;">You might think this is just a digression about history and the gods, but it's actually nothing more than a recipe for disaster. Let's try it!</span></p>

<summary><strong>Library as Memory Palace</strong><br />Information technologies and mnemonic techniques&nbsp;</summary>
<p><img style="float: right; padding-left: 10px;" src="https://iiif.library.ucla.edu/iiif/2/ark%3A%2F21198%2Fz1hj2vr1/full/600,/0/default.jpg" alt="Archival black and white photograph from 1964 showing card catalogs at the UCLA University Research Library" width="50%" height="50%" />In its most obvious form, a research library is a building with books and other materials that is staffed by librarians who help patrons find information they need for various purposes. It also provides study spaces, instructional classrooms, lending technology, and workshops, even a cafe. Libraries also contain offices, processing facilities, and storage areas that are off-limits to patrons. Since the introduction of digital technologies, libraries have increasingly turned to online systems to make information available to patrons; over time, user access points have moved from computer terminals in the building, to remote access through devices owned by users, which does not require they be inside the building.</p>
<p>Because libraries evolve with changes in how information is collected, stored, and accessed, they also leave a trace through time of many different kinds of material, from 1000 year-old manuscripts to datasets from this year. Some of the physical infrastructure may be replaced, and some remain as originally constructed. And the patrons who use the library also represent a demographic cross-section of generations. Older visitors may browse and value book stacks, while newer users might prefer online resources and rarely step foot in the library, if only for studying and social gathering.&nbsp;</p>
<p>How people understand and use the library, in other words, depends largely upon the mental map of information they imprinted in their brains and bodies through spatial, visual, verbal, and experiential memory. In that sense, a library is not just a place in the physical and virtual world. It is also a place in human memory, subject to the complex, but also regular systems by which people organize, store and access information in their own heads.</p>

<summary><strong>IIIF Manifest</strong><br />The IIIF manifest can look scary, but it holds the key to a world of high quality content</summary>
<p><span style="font-size: 12pt;">IIIF is officially pronounced "triple-eye-eff." The global community of librarians, archivists, museum curators, technologists, and educators who envisioned the <strong>International Image Interoperability Framework</strong> bypassed emoji &nbsp;(<span style="font-size: 14pt;"><sup>👁👁👁</sup><strong>f</strong></span>) and conjured up a logo <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e8/International_Image_Interoperability_Framework_logo.png" alt="IIIF logo" width="30" height="30" /> with awesome powers. A manifest is an online file or list of information about a digital object, such as metadata (title, artist, holding institution, etc) and links to images, videos, audio files, or even 3d renderings. </span><span style="font-size: 12pt;">To use it, all you need is the URL, which is typically linked from the <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e8/International_Image_Interoperability_Framework_logo.png" alt="IIIF logo" width="30" height="30" /> logo. You copy and paste it into various IIIF-compatible tools to load items for use.</span></p>
