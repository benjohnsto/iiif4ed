## {{page.title}}

{%- include heading name="concept" -%}


<p><img role="presentation" src="https://media.getty.edu/iiif/image/c567e821-2389-4e7c-b474-b4734efd6a29/1004,2893,2073,1346/1400,/0/default.jpg" alt="" width="100%" height="100%" /></p>
<p>In the <a class="inline_disabled" href="https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/book/12/folio/iv?spTexts=2&amp;nhTexts=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Prologue to Book 12</a>, Fray Bernardino de Sahag&uacute;n explained that the Nahuatl-language narrative was based on interviews with Nahua elders who had experienced the war. <span style="font-family: 'Lato Extended', 'Helvetica Neue', Helvetica, Arial, sans-serif;">He attested to the value of the indigenous account since &ldquo;those who were conquered knew and gave an account of many things that transpired among them during the war of which those who conquered them were unaware.&rdquo; He acknowledged the ways that Nahuas kept memories of their experiences of the war alive through oral histories. One fascinating aspect of Nahua social memory is their knowledge of certain war heroes.</span></p>


{%- include heading name="activity" -%}


<p>We will conduct searches within the Digital Florentine Codex on some of the earliest representations of Mexica heroes in the Florentine Codex to better understand how information in Book 12 can shed light on other colonial texts and images of the conquest. Our shared images and comments will generate further discussion.</p>

<details style="color: #000000;">
<summary><strong>Visual memory in imagery<br /></strong>Tlappanecatl Ehcatzin captures the Spanish banner (f. 67r) <span style="color: #236fa1;"><em>(click to open and close)</em></span><br /><img src="https://media.getty.edu/iiif/image/325cdd9e-014d-45dc-aea4-d84ade9581dc/331,2166,1901,1516/,300/0/default.jpg" alt="Detail of a page from the Florentine Codex, Book 12" width="12%" height="12%" /><br /><strong></strong></summary>
<p>The image below reveals memories of a hero named Tlappanecatl Ehcatzin who captured the Spanish banner. This is represented as a pivotal and highly symbolic moment that grants the Mexica a victory, even if they don't win the war. It is doubtful that Sahag&uacute;n or other Spaniards would have remembered this warrior's name decades after the conquest; thus, the detail reveals a distinct indigenous memory that, in Sahag&uacute;n's words,</p>
<p style="padding-left: 40px;"><em>&ldquo;those who were conquered knew and gave an account of many things that transpired among them during the war of which those who conquered them were unaware.&rdquo; </em></p>
<p><strong>Note</strong> that the tlacuilo used a Mesoamerican pictorial writing convention of using <em>size</em> to indicate status and importance. Here Tlappanecatl Ehcatzin towers over the Spaniards as he wrests the banner from them.</p>
