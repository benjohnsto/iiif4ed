## {{page.title}}


{%- include heading name="concept" -%}


<p>In the 19th century, before the widespread acceptance of germ theory, public health officials grappled with devastating cholera epidemics. To understand these outbreaks, they turned to spatial data visualization. While John Snow's 1854 map of the Broad Street pump is famous for isolating a waterborne source, earlier efforts&mdash;like Edwin Chadwick's sanitary map of Leeds and Boston's 1849 cholera tracking&mdash;reveal how officials tried to correlate disease with the environment, poverty, and sanitation. Often, these maps were designed to prove the "miasma" theory (that disease was spread by foul air from poor drainage) rather than tainted water.</p>

<summary style="cursor: pointer;">Text-Based Visual Description (For Screen Readers)</summary>
<p style="margin-top: 10px;"><strong>Chadwick's Sanitary Map of Leeds:</strong> A dense, color-coded street map of Leeds, England. The map uses shaded zones to indicate the varying living conditions and sanitation levels of different neighborhoods, heavily emphasizing areas with poor drainage and "foul air," reflecting the miasma theory of disease transmission.</p>
<p><strong>Course of Cholera in Boston (1849):</strong> A map of the Boston peninsula focusing on the 1849 outbreak. It uses distinct visual markers to plot individual cases or clusters of cholera across the city's wards, highlighting the spatial relationship between the disease and the city's contemporary waterfronts and crowded districts.</p>
