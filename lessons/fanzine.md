## {{page.title}}


{%- include heading name="concept" -%}

<p>One of the most recent phenomena of the pop culture melting pot are fanzines. A fanzine is something akin to an underground magazine, and there are virtually thousands of them springing up all over the country.</p><p>&ldquo;Fanzines cover a wide spectrum of ideas; the articles, interviews and band reviews, however, are not written in a style found in conventional media. The majority of fanzines are put together in a chaotic fashion; poetry, art dabblings, discussions of social and economic repression and articles about non-mainstream bands abound.&rdquo;</p>

{%- include heading name="activity" -%}

<p>In this activity we will study the relationship between image and text. I am including a kind of Fanzine titled "Brujas" (or "Witches", in English), which was published in Argentina, in 1983, after the return of democracy. Its focus is the social condition of women in the society in two areas: sexual violence and labor rights, especially domestic work. </p>
<p>In addition, I proposed the analysis of Mujer/Fempress, which discusses the feminist movement, the rights and socioeconomic situation of women in Latin America, and two similar documents by Margaret Cuischank, American lesbian feminist writer and academic. </p><p>Students can choose any of these works to select an image with the "IIIF Cropping Tool" and write a brief (100-200 word post) about the relationship between the image and the text that surrounds it. </p>


<h4>Objectives</h4>

<ul>
  <li>understand the functionality of the "IIIF cropping tool", capturing small pieces of content </li>
  <li>expand our conception of community organizing, the feminist movement in Latin America, and identify fanzine elements</li>
</ul>

{%- include heading name="example" -%}


<p>In this image, you can see how a designer created a skewed world-vision where men constitute the workforce predominantly. Not only that, in this picture one can appreciate a male figure raising his hand, as a symbol of participation. The author in the fanzine argues that this image also portrays only white-collar workers; emphasizes labor/class inequalities in which employees benefit from workers. She also mentions a historical disparity that began to change not long ago, in 1940 (in Argentina). Finally, we can also expand this discussion to other areas, such as family roles, property rights and obligations, and question what we really mean when we talk about the universality of Human Rights. </p>
