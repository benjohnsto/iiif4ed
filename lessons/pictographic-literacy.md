<p>Elizabeth Hill Boone is a scholar and expert on pictographic languages. She writes:</p>
<div class="page" title="Page 2">
<div class="section">
<div class="layoutArea">
<div class="column">
<p style="padding-left: 40px;"><em>As an Aztec specialist, I&nbsp;argue for a broader, more encompassing definition of writing, one that embraces nonverbal systems. Several of my colleagues, people whom I respect and whose opinions I trust, ask me why we need to do this, when such a broadening blurs the important distinction between phonetic writing and other forms. It is because the word "writing" is so loaded. Writing is not merely a type of notational system but an entire cultural category. It has been used to distinguish literate people from preliterates, people with history from those without, and even civilized people from barbarians or primitives. Writing, as a "cornerstone of the human heritage," to quote Schoville and Senner, is seen as a basic clement of civilized society. Gelb represents this prevalent position when he states, "Writing exists only in a civilization and a civilization cannot exist without writing." Given these meanings, how can we deny that the Aztecs and Mixtecs had writing? </em></p>

<p>Boone recognizes, in other words, that writing can take many forms and communicate information through various channels, or modalities both visual and verbal. &nbsp;</p>


{%- include heading name="activity" -%}


<p>For this lab activity, we will read, view, and consider Boone's scholarly argument about the nature of pictographic communication as form of writing that does not necessarily involve verbal speech as a modality. Following her examples in a video-recorded lecture, we will closely inspect several Mesoamerican codices. Then we will apply her "readings" to illustrated pages of the Florentine Codex to speculate on whether, and/or to what degree, imagery in that document communicates pictographic meanings in addition to its ostensive function of naturalistic visual depiction of people, places, animals, things, and events. We will look for examples of the three writing techniques discussed by Boone that are used in the Florentine Codex: direct depiction; ideograms; and phonetic referents (glyphs), and we will browse other select materials to find examples for comparison.</p>
